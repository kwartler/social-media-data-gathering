SPEAKER NOTES: RESEARCH ETHICS FOR SOCIAL MEDIA TEXT DATA
Learn NLP Research Methods Applied to Social Media
Companion to the social-media-data-gathering app

Use these notes alongside a live demo of the app. Each section is one talking
point, roughly one slide, with a suggested prompt for discussion.


1. PUBLIC IS NOT THE SAME AS FAIR GAME

- A post being visible without a login does not mean its author agreed to be
  studied, quoted, or fed to an AI model.
- People post for an audience they imagine (friends, followers, a hashtag
  community), not for a researcher's corpus. Moving their words into a new
  context can harm them even when nothing was technically private.
- The question to ask is not "can I collect this?" but "would a reasonable
  person expect their post to be used this way, and could it hurt them?"

Discussion: Is a TikTok with 200 views "public" in the same way as a news
channel with 2 million subscribers?


2. WHO ARE YOU STUDYING, AND WHO IS A BYSTANDER?

- Studying a public organization or public figure in their public role (for
  example, the YouTube channel Channel5News) is different from studying private
  individuals.
- The app reflects this with three Pseudonymize settings:
    "Commenters and @mentions only": keep the names of the accounts you chose
      to study; replace everyone else with codes. Default on the YouTube tab.
    "Everyone": replace every name, including post authors. Default on TikTok,
      Instagram, Facebook, Reddit, and Bluesky, where post authors are often
      private individuals, and some may be minors.
    "No one": keep all names. Use only when every person in the data is a
      public account acting publicly, and your protocol allows it.
- Commenters are almost always bystanders. They replied to a video; they did
  not sign up to be research subjects.

Discussion: You study a politician's YouTube channel. Are the people in the
comments part of your study or bystanders? What changes if a commenter is
quoted in your paper?


3. PSEUDONYMIZED IS NOT ANONYMOUS

- The app replaces names with codes such as u_3f9a1c02de, and replaces @mentions
  inside the text too. The same person always gets the same code, so you can
  still count posts per author or map who replies to whom.
- But the content itself can identify people: a quote typed into a search
  engine often finds the original post in seconds. A video description can
  mention a name on a sign, a school logo, or a distinctive tattoo.
- In publications, paraphrase rather than quote verbatim when a quote could
  lead back to a private person, and never publish codes alongside quotes that
  can be searched.

Discussion: Take one sentence from your corpus and search it. Can you find the
author?


4. THE LINKING KEY: SEPARATE, PRIVATE, TEMPORARY

- Every export puts the key that maps codes back to real names and links in a
  separate folder, identifiable/. That folder is included by default because
  you need it to check the model's work and to honor deletion requests.
- Treat it like a key to a locked room:
    Store it apart from the analysis files, with tighter access.
    Share the analysis data (corpus.jsonl) with collaborators; never the key.
    Delete it on the schedule your protocol sets.
- restore_identities.R puts names back for a specific task, such as manual
  review. The output file contains real names and must stay private too.

Discussion: Where on your laptop is the key right now? Who else could open it?


5. MODEL OUTPUT IS A MEASUREMENT, NOT THE TRUTH

- Transcripts, speaker labels, on-screen text, visual descriptions, and
  translations from the model are AI-generated and unverified. Every export
  marks them with ai_generated = true.
- Speaker labels in context: in a 2026 benchmark of long, multi-speaker
  recordings in 22 Indian languages, a leading model misattributed about 33% of
  words to the wrong speaker. That is a hard test, and dedicated commercial
  transcription services scored 33 to 44% on it. Short social clips with one or
  two speakers are likely easier, but nobody has measured that, so we check.
- Model timestamps are approximate, and labels are per video: speaker "A" in one
  video has nothing to do with speaker "A" in another.
- Translations miss slang, sarcasm, and code-switching most often. Analyze
  originals and translations separately, and say which one you used.
- The practice: run review_sheet.R, hand-check a random sample (20 posts is a
  reasonable start), and report the result, for example "transcripts were fully
  correct for 17 of 20 sampled posts."

Discussion: The platform's captions and the model's transcript disagree on the
same video. Which one is "right," and how would you find out?


6. REPRODUCIBILITY AND PROVENANCE

- manifest.json records every link submitted, every failure and its reason, the
  settings used, the exact model, the full prompt with its version number, and
  the cost. Your methods section should report the model and prompt version.
- The collection log records every attempt, including failures. Failed posts
  are part of your sampling story: if private or deleted posts fail more often,
  your sample is biased toward content that stayed public.
- Engagement counts (likes, views) are a snapshot at collection time, and posts
  can be edited or deleted later. Report when you collected.

Discussion: You collected 500 posts and 60 failed. What do you write in your
methods section?


7. PLATFORM RULES AND THE LAW

- Reddit and Bluesky provide official APIs for this kind of collection.
  YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, and Facebook collection reads public pages, which
  their terms of service may restrict. Know which you are using.
- The app never logs in to any platform to reach private content, and it does
  not support collecting whole Instagram or Facebook profiles for that reason.
- Research you plan to publish or share needs IRB approval or a documented
  exemption before you collect, not after.

Discussion: Does a platform's terms of service change what is ethical, or only
what is permitted?


8. HARMFUL AND ILLEGAL CONTENT

- Social media data can contain violence, hate, self-harm, and sexual content.
  Plan for researcher wellbeing: take breaks, and do not review disturbing
  content alone for long stretches.
- If you encounter content that sexualizes minors: stop, do not save, copy, or
  share it, tell your instructor, and report it to NCMEC at
  https://report.cybertip.org. Possessing it is a crime regardless of intent.
- By default the app deletes downloaded videos and images after the model reads
  them. Leave "Keep downloaded media files" off unless you have a specific need.

Discussion: Whose job is it to decide a video is too harmful to analyze?


9. COLLECT LESS, KEEP LESS

- Collect only what your research question needs. If you do not need comments,
  leave comments at 0. If you do not need names, keep pseudonymization on.
- Everything you keep is something that can leak. Decide your deletion date at
  the start of the project and write it down.

Closing prompt: For your own project, write one sentence each on who you are
studying, who the bystanders are, what you will do with the linking key, and
when you will delete the data.
